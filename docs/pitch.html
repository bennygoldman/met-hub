MET Hub

A proposal for one place where MET Radio's hosts and staff do their work, built on top of the tools the station already uses.

Why

The station runs on several decent tools: Airtime for playout and scheduling, Spinitron for playlists and logs, Airtable and Google Drive for the music library, WordPress for the website and charts. None of them share information, so people move it between them by hand.

That shows up as recurring scheduling work every week, logs typed in after the fact, and new music that's hard to actually listen to before it goes to air. Hosts also have no shared place for the station itself, its people, its shows and what's going on around it.

The idea

MET Hub is a website with sign-in for hosts and staff. It doesn't try to replace everything at once. It sits in front of proven tools, connects them, and gives people a simpler screen for each job.

Two views

Hosts and staff sign in to the same site but see different things. Hosts get their show, the crate and the community. Staff get those plus the station tools. Nothing in the host view requires knowing how the schedule is built.

The first page anyone sees after signing in. It's about the station, not the software: what people made this week, who's new, what's coming up.

The only personal item is a single line about your own show, and only when something needs doing.

Highlights are segments hosts choose to share from their own episodes.

The crate

The station's music library, rebuilt as somewhere you can listen. It replaces the Airtable list and the Drive folders.

Every release the station has been sent and cleared for air lives here. Signed-in hosts can stream any of it in the browser, so sampling an album takes one click instead of a download.

Browsing is by release, not by track. Pick an album to hear it, open the details to see the music director's notes and who has played it, then save it or add a track to an episode you're building.

The filters lean on what the station knows that streaming services don't: what's new to the station, what's been played here, what's been overlooked, and what's Canadian or local.

Chart entries are real (MET Top 30, Nov 10, 2026). Origins, genres, notes and play counts are placeholders.

The log

A plain table that mostly fills itself. Anything played from the crate or by the automation is already known. Music from a turntable or a laptop is identified from the air feed when possible.

The host fills in whatever is left blank, checks the totals and finishes the log. The same records feed the charts and the station's reports, so nobody re-types them.

Schedule

Recurring scheduling work becomes a short list of rules the station writes once. Each week the hub drafts the schedule from them, a person reviews the result, and it goes to playout.

The weekly check replaces hunting through the calendar for problems.

Intake and charts

New music arrives in one queue no matter how it was sent. Once reviewed, it appears in the crate for hosts.

The chart is drafted from the logs. Publishing it updates the website, a public playlist and the newsletter at once.

What it's built from

The hub itself is a small web app with one database. The heavy parts are existing tools:

JobTool
Playout, stream and the on-air calendarLibreTime, the open-source project Airtime came from. Same ideas, and the hub can write to its schedule.
Identifying music on live showsAn audio fingerprinting service, or open-source fingerprinting matched against the station's own library.
Audio filesCloud storage, streamed only to signed-in accounts.
Release informationMusicBrainz and Discogs.
Public outputsThe existing website, Spotify, and the chart services the station already reports to.

Prior work

TPS was a logging, library and compliance tool for Canadian campus and community radio built for CKXU in Lethbridge around 2013.

It got a lot right. Compliance targets were set per genre and per program. Each log entry carried the fields regulators ask for. Ads and PSAs due in the hour appeared inside the log. The station chart was calculated from logs instead of tallied by hand, and the library had a proper intake and review process.

Where it fell short is where this proposal starts: every song was entered by hand, it was only ever an admin tool, and each station ran its own copy. Its data model is a useful checklist.

How it would roll out

One piece at a time, each running next to the tool it replaces until the station trusts it.

Read-only first. Home page and crate on top of the current Airtable and Airtime data. Nothing changes for anyone.
Library. Music moves out of Airtable and Drive into the crate.
Playout and scheduling. LibreTime runs alongside Airtime, then takes over, with scheduling rules.
Logging. Logs and charts move into the hub, replacing Spinitron.
Other stations. If it works for MET, partner stations could run their own version and share music and charts.
Draft, November 2026.
